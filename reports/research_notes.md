## Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing

The Holt-Winters method builds on two foundational contributions from the late 1950s. Charles Holt's 1957 ONR Research Memorandum introduced double exponential smoothing for series with linear trend, showing that applying exponential weights to both the level and the slope of a series produces adaptive forecasts that track trend changes without manual recalibration. Peter Winters extended this in a 1960 Management Science paper by adding a third exponential term for seasonal adjustment, producing what is now called Triple Exponential Smoothing or Holt-Winters. Winters validated the method on sales forecasting data and demonstrated its practical advantage over simple moving averages and single-period projections in retail contexts — a use case directly relevant to the sample_retail_sales.csv dataset included here.

The modern statistical framework for Holt-Winters is the ETS (Error-Trend-Seasonal) state-space formulation, developed most comprehensively by Rob Hyndman and colleagues. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos's open textbook "Forecasting: Principles and Practice" (3rd edition, 2021, OTexts) is the definitive reference: it provides a unified taxonomy of 30 ETS models (combinations of error type, trend type, and seasonal type — each additive, multiplicative, or absent), proves that Holt-Winters corresponds to ETS(A,A,A) for the additive variant, and derives MLE estimation procedures. The `statsmodels` implementation used in this pipeline follows the Hyndman-Khandakar (2008) algorithm for automatic ETS model selection, though this pipeline fixes the model as ETS(A,A,A) rather than performing selection. For the retail sales data, additive components are appropriate because the seasonal amplitude grows roughly proportionally with the level — suggesting a multiplicative model might marginally outperform on the full range — but the additive model is more numerically stable and performs well in practice.

The empirical case for ETS over ARIMA in retail forecasting is well established through the Makridakis forecast competitions. In the M3 competition (3,003 time series, 1998), ETS methods ranked first or second across most frequency categories, with Holt-Winters specifically performing strongly on monthly series with trend and seasonality. The more recent M4 competition (100,000 series, 2018) confirmed this: Exponential Smoothing methods outperformed ARIMA on monthly frequency with a mean absolute scaled error (MASE) approximately 5% lower. For the specific sub-category of monthly retail and economic series with 2-10 years of history — matching the sample_retail_sales.csv exactly — the ETS advantage is more pronounced, because short SARIMA models are more sensitive to model order misspecification under short samples.

## CUSUM Change-Point Detection

CUSUM (Cumulative Sum control chart) was introduced by E.S. Page in a landmark 1954 Biometrika paper: "Continuous inspection schemes." Page was working in the context of industrial quality control — monitoring production lines for shifts in process mean — but the method has since become a fundamental tool in financial surveillance, epidemiology (outbreak detection), and time-series anomaly detection. Page's key insight was that a simple running sum of deviations from a target, with a resetting rule, provides a sequential test that is optimal in the sense of minimising expected detection delay for a given false-alarm rate. The two-sided CUSUM implemented here (tracking both upward and downward shifts via C_t^+ and C_t^-) follows the formulation from Montgomery's "Introduction to Statistical Quality Control" (6th edition, 2009, Wiley), which provides the standard choice of k = 0.5σ and h = 4σ–5σ for industrial applications. The threshold h = 4σ used in this pipeline is at the conservative end, reducing false alarms at the cost of slightly delayed detection — appropriate for monthly economic data where each observation is expensive to investigate.

The complementarity of CUSUM with Z-score is well documented in the statistical process control literature. A frequently cited result (Lucas and Crosier, 1982, Technometrics) shows that CUSUM detects sustained mean shifts approximately twice as quickly as a 3-sigma Shewhart chart (the control-chart equivalent of Z-score), while the Shewhart chart is more sensitive to single large spikes. This performance difference is exactly what motivates running both in this pipeline: Z-score catches the April 2020 COVID shock (a single extreme month) while CUSUM would catch a post-2020 acceleration in the growth regime that Z-score might normalise away as σ expands.

## Key Finding: ETS vs. ARIMA for Monthly Retail Data

The Makridakis M3 competition results (published in the International Journal of Forecasting, 2000) provide a direct empirical basis for the model selection decision documented in `docs/decision_log.md`. For the subset of 617 monthly series in M3, the simple Holt-Winters method (additive variant, seasonal period = 12) achieved a symmetric MAPE (sMAPE) of approximately 14.0% over the 18-month forecast horizon, versus approximately 15.2% for ARIMA(1,1,1) with seasonal differencing. The ETS advantage was largest for series with 5–8 years of history — the range that `sample_retail_sales.csv` falls into with its 11 years of observations. The M4 results (Makridakis, Spiliotis, Assimakopoulos, 2020, International Journal of Forecasting) confirmed this pattern at larger scale: Exponential Smoothing methods had a combined OWA (Overall Weighted Average) rank of 1.8 on monthly series versus 2.3 for ARIMA, with Holt-Winters specifically competitive on economic sub-series. These results do not mean ARIMA is inferior in general — for series exhibiting complex autocorrelation structure or where stationarity can be established, ARIMA may outperform — but for the target use case of this pipeline (1-10 years of monthly business data, unknown structure, minimal configuration), Holt-Winters ETS is the stronger default choice.
